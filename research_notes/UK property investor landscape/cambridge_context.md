Method note for the report writer: research ran on 28 Sep 2026. ## 1. Prices, volumes, £/m², time on market, and house vs flat stock

### Takeaway
Cambridge city prices have been flat to slightly down since the 2022 peak. The ONS/Land Registry HPI average was £475,000 in July 2026, level with July 2025, after falling 2.5% in the year to December 2025. Semis are rising slightly and flats are falling. Central CB1 sectors trade at about £6,750–£6,770/m² and CB4 1 at about £5,760/m². The market is described as "broadly flat" and "balanced", with about 13 weeks to go under offer.

### Inferences
- Nominal prices have been flat for 2024–26, so a flip in CB1–CB5 cannot rely on market growth. Margin has to come from the value-add itself (refurb, extra space, planning gain).
- Flats are falling (−2.3%) while semis rise (+1.3%). Together with Bidwells' 2025 note that houses fell while flats rose, the sub-markets are rotating. Houses with extension or garden potential look like the more resilient target in 2026.
- The spread in £/m² between central CB1 sectors (~£6.75k) and CB1 8 or CB4 (~£5.6–5.8k) is large enough that fine-grained, sector-level comparables matter for a screening tool. District averages will mislead.
- Portal averages (£571k–£611k) run well above the ONS HPI (£475k) because of mix-adjustment and geography. The tool should use Land Registry price-paid data with its own £/m² (via EPC floor areas) rather than headline averages.

### Gaps

- District-level (CB1, CB2, CB3, CB4, CB5) averages and £/m² from one consistent source and date could not be obtained. Pages were blocked, and the snippets mix sectors and districts.
- Detached and terraced annual change for July 2026, and yearly averages for 2022 and 2023, were not captured.
- No Census 2021 accommodation-type split (houses vs flats) for Cambridge city could be retrieved. The search budget ran out before this query.

## 2. Rental market: rents, yields, students/HMOs, licensing, Article 4, landlord activity

### Takeaway
Cambridge is the most expensive place to rent in Cambridgeshire: £1,805 pcm (ONS, August 2026), up only 1.8% year on year. Gross yields are low, mostly about 3.1–5%. Bidwells reports investor yields at a decade high of 5.0–5.1% as prices soften and some landlords sell ahead of the Renters' Rights Act. Tighter HMO rules are coming: after a July 2026 petition the council agreed cross-party to pursue an Article 4 direction on small (3–6 person) HMOs, with a cabinet report due autumn 2026.

### Cited Findings
**Rents**
- ONS: average private rent in Cambridge **£1,805 pcm in August 2026**, up **1.8%** from £1,773 in August 2025. East of England £1,289 (from £1,245); UK £1,400 (from £1,348) — [ONS Housing prices in Cambridge](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/housingpriceslocal/E07000008/)
- Cambridge News (21 Sep 2026): Cambridge rents are **£405/month above the UK average**. The city is the most expensive of Cambridgeshire's six districts, but its annual increase was smaller than most neighbours' — [Cambridge News via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/uk/cambridge-news/20260921/281573772603329)
- Home.co.uk: average asking rent **£1,785 pcm**. Rental median time on market **48 days**; asking rents range from £298 to £3,750 pcm (data to 26 Aug 2026) — [Home.co.uk rental prices Cambridge](https://home.co.uk/rental-prices/location/cambridge/current/)

**Bidwells quarterly rental reports**
- Spring 2026: **prime flat rents +2.3% year on year**; **average yields at a decade high of 5.1%**. Q1 2026 marks "a return to momentum" as demand rebounds and "greater regulatory clarity restores confidence". The Renters' Rights Act is "reshaping landlord strategy" — [Bidwells Spring 2026](https://www.bidwells.co.uk/insights-reports-events/cambridge-residential-rental-market-report-spring-2026/)
- Autumn 2025: **mainstream rents +3.75%**, prime flat rents +2.3%, and **investor yields at a decade-high 5.0% "as buy-to-let landlords exit ahead of the Renters' Rights Act"**. Demand is driven by tech, science and medical professionals — [Bidwells Autumn 2025](https://homes.bidwells.co.uk/insights-reports/cambridge-residential-rental-market-report-autumn-2025/)
- Summer 2025: two-bed apartment rents **+8.2%** — [Bidwells Summer 2025](https://homes.bidwells.co.uk/market-insights/cambridge-residential-rental-market-report-summer-2025/)

**Yields by postcode (portal and advisory estimates, 2026)**
- Gross yields in Cambridge city postcodes range about **3.1%–4.7%** across ten postcodes: "lower than most of the country, because the price is so high" — [PropertyInvestmentsUK "Yields of 4.7%"](https://www.propertyinvestmentsuk.co.uk/cambridge-buy-to-let/)
- **CB1 about 3.6%**; **CB4 (Chesterton/North Cambridge) about 4.3%**, with entry prices around £425,000 — [PropertyInvestmentsUK](https://www.propertyinvestmentsuk.co.uk/cambridge-buy-to-let/); [CIA Landlords Cambridge postcodes](https://www.cia-landlords.co.uk/area-guides/the-best-cambridge-postcodes-for-buy-to-let-success/)
- **CB5**: "often exceed 5.0%" per [Belvoir CB5 guide](https://www.belvoir.co.uk/guides/cambridge/cb5-cambridge-area-guide-everything-you-need-to-know/); **4.4%** per [Morris Armitage 2026](https://morrisarmitage.co.uk/rental-yields-cambridge-east-anglia/). The two sources conflict.

**HMO licensing and Article 4**
- Mandatory HMO licensing applies to properties with **5+ occupants** forming more than one household. The **licence fee is £1,133** — [Belvoir HMO licensing Cambridge](https://www.belvoir.co.uk/guides/cambridge/hmo-licensing-in-cambridge-do-you-need-a-licence/); [Cambridge City Council HMO licensing](https://www.cambridge.gov.uk/licensing-of-houses-in-multiple-occupation)
- Currently planning permission is needed only to convert to a **"large" HMO of 7+ occupants** (sui generis). Small HMOs (3–6) are permitted development — [Cambridge City Council ePetition](https://democracy.cambridge.gov.uk/mgEPetitionDisplay.aspx?id=112)
- An ePetition ("Introduce fair limits and control the spread of HMOs in Cambridge") ran **12 May–16 July 2026** and gathered 500+ signatures, triggering a full council debate on **16 July 2026**. It asks for (1) a Local Plan **10% HMO threshold within a 100m radius**, and (2) an **Article 4 direction** requiring permission for small HMOs (3–6 people) — [ePetition](https://democracy.cambridge.gov.uk/mgEPetitionDisplay.aspx?id=112); [Change.org petition](https://www.change.org/p/introduce-fair-limits-and-control-the-spread-of-hmos-in-cambridge)
- At that meeting councillors **agreed cross-party** that action was needed. The Labour leader called Article 4 "a highly important tool". Officers will bring a **cabinet report in autumn 2026** to "map out" implementation. Greater Cambridge Shared Planning is reviewing the Article 4 position — [Cambridge Independent: HMO crackdown earns cross-party consensus](https://www.cambridgeindependent.co.uk/news/hmo-crackdown-in-cambridge-earns-cross-party-consensus-9475511/); [Cambridge Independent: campaigners welcome promise](https://www.cambridgeindependent.co.uk/news/campaigners-welcome-promise-of-action-to-limit-hmos-in-cambr-9471277/); [Ely Standard](https://www.elystandard.co.uk/news/26288072.cambridge-council-considers-hmo-restrictions-petition/)
- Conflict: a Belvoir guide says Cambridge "operates Article 4 Directions across a number of areas" that remove permitted development for shared housing — [Belvoir](https://www.belvoir.co.uk/guides/cambridge/do-i-need-an-hmo-licence-in-cambridge-rules-and-requirements/). This is contradicted by the petition and council coverage, which describe the HMO Article 4 as not yet in place (July 2026) — [Cambridge Independent](https://www.cambridgeindependent.co.uk/news/hmo-crackdown-in-cambridge-earns-cross-party-consensus-9475511/). Treat Belvoir's claim as unreliable.
- A council HMO appendix (older, undated in results) and a community map of HMOs exist — [Appendix 1: HMOs in Cambridge](https://democracy.cambridge.gov.uk/documents/s19893/HMO%20-%20Ap%201.pdf); [cambridgehmos.netlify.app](https://cambridgehmos.netlify.app/)
- Private Rented Sector Housing Standard (council) — [Cambridge PRS Housing Standard](https://www.cambridge.gov.uk/media/5636/private-rented-sector-housing-standard.pdf)

### Inferences
- A £1,805 pcm average rent against a £475k average price gives about **4.6% gross** (computed: 1,805 × 12 / 475,000). This matches the 3–5% range above. Plain buy-to-let in CB1–CB3 is thin, so value-add (extra bedrooms, loft or extension, splitting into flats where permitted) matters more for landlords.
- An Article 4 on C3→C4 is likely to be made in 2027 or later. From general planning practice (not sourced here), councils usually give 12 months' notice to avoid compensation liability, but Cambridge's timing is unconfirmed. It would remove the "family house to small HMO without planning" route that student-area investors use (CB1 Mill Road/Romsey and CB4 are typical). Screening should flag HMO-conversion upside as **policy-at-risk** from autumn 2026.
- Decade-high yields plus reports of landlords leaving suggest a supply of tired ex-rental stock, which suits refurb or flip buyers.

### Gaps
- No verified figures for University of Cambridge or Anglia Ruskin student numbers, purpose-built student accommodation supply, or the number of licensed HMOs in the city.
- Rent levels by bedroom count (ONS) could not be retrieved.
- Whether any Article 4 directions (for example on other permitted development rights in conservation areas) already apply in Cambridge could not be checked.
- Selective or additional HMO licensing scheme status could not be confirmed. Nothing beyond mandatory licensing surfaced.

## 3. Planning: Policy 52 (garden land), emerging GCLP Policy H/GL and timetable, conservation areas, service performance

### Takeaway
Garden-plot and backland schemes in the city are tested against Cambridge Local Plan 2018 Policy 52. It permits garden or plot-subdivision development only where specific character, amenity and comprehensive-development tests are met. The emerging Greater Cambridge Local Plan carries this forward almost unchanged as Policy H/GL. The Proposed Submission (Regulation 19) consultation ran 3 Aug–25 Sep 2026, submission is due by end-December 2026 and examination is expected in early 2027. Cambridge has 17–18 conservation areas. No hard data on service performance was found.

### Cited Findings
- Policy 52 (Cambridge Local Plan 2018) wording: "Proposals for development on sites that form part of a garden or group of gardens or that subdivide an existing residential plot will only be permitted where…" (criteria follow) — [Cambridge Local Plan 2018 PDF](https://cambridge.gov.uk/media/6890/local-plan-2018.pdf); applied in committee reports, e.g. [18/1685/FUL report](https://democracy.cambridge.gov.uk/documents/s45840/181685FUL%20-%20Report.pdf) and [23/02094/FUL – 5 Hinton Avenue](https://democracy.cambridge.gov.uk/documents/s63911/23-02094-FUL%20-%205%20Hinton%20Avenue.pdf)
- Emerging **Policy H/GL: Garden land and subdivision of existing plots** "continue[s] the approach of the adopted Cambridge and South Cambridgeshire Local Plans". It resists inappropriate garden development and plot subdivision "by reference to a range of factors including potential harm to local character, adequacy of remaining amenity space, trees, heritage and biodiversity". It will also "stop development that would compromise the potential of other land to be brought forward" — [GCSP First Proposals: Policy H/GL](https://consultations.greatercambridgeplanning.org/greater-cambridge-local-plan-first-proposals/explore-theme/homes/policy-hgl-garden-land-and)
- H/GL rationale: gardens "contribute to local character and mitigate flooding" but also give "scope for new residential development without losing countryside". Consultation responses supported controlling garden use, "in particular the paving over of front gardens" (biodiversity, urban cooling) — [GCSP Policy H/GL](https://consultations.greatercambridgeplanning.org/greater-cambridge-local-plan-first-proposals/explore-theme/homes/policy-hgl-garden-land-and); [Homes Topic Paper Oct 2025](https://consultations.greatercambridgeplanning.org/sites/gcp/files/2025-10/TPGCLPDGCLPHTPOct25.pdf)
- A Draft GCLP (Regulation 18) consultation was held in **late 2025** (documents dated Nov 2025) — [Draft GCLP Reg 18 document](https://consultations.greatercambridgeplanning.org/sites/gcp/files/2025-11/PDGCLPPDGCLP1IDSNov25.pdf); [GCSP Draft GCLP consultation](https://consultations.greatercambridgeplanning.org/draft-greater-cambridge-local-plan-consultation)
- The updated plan was published on **8 July 2026** ahead of the final consultation — [Cambridge City Council news 8 Jul 2026](https://www.cambridge.gov.uk/news/2026/07/08/updated-greater-cambridge-local-plan-published-ahead-of-final-public-consultation); [SCDC news](https://www.scambs.gov.uk/news/updated-greater-cambridge-local-plan-published-ahead-of-final-public-consultation)
- The **Proposed Submission** consultation ran **3 August–25 September 2026** and is limited to legal compliance and "soundness". **Submission by end of December 2026** meets the government's cut-off for plans examined under the current system. **Examination is expected to begin in early 2027** — [GCSP Proposed Submission GCLP](https://consultations.greatercambridgeplanning.org/proposed-submission-greater-cambridge-local-plan); [LDS decision](https://democracy.cambridge.gov.uk/ieDecisionDetails.aspx?Id=5694); [LDS report](https://democracy.cambridge.gov.uk/documents/s67659/2501-LP%20timetable%20PTSC%20report%20v2.pdf)
- Conservation areas: Cambridge City Council has **18** — [GCSP conservation areas](https://www.greatercambridgeplanning.org/heritage-and-conservation/conservation-areas); [Cambridge PPF](https://cambridgeppf.org/conservation-areas-buildings-of-local-interest/). One architect's guide says **17**, noting the large Central Core CA has been split into smaller areas — [Inclume Architects guide](https://www.inclume.co.uk/journal/guide-to-conservation-areas-in-cambridge). Source data: [Cambridge CC conservation areas GIS](https://3csharedservicesopendata-cambridgeonline.hub.arcgis.com/datasets/cambridge-city-council-conservation-areas); [land area by CA dataset](https://data.cambridgeshireinsight.org.uk/dataset/analysis-and-interpretation-tree-audit-data-cambridge-city-council/resource/981f01aa-81ad)
- The Greater Cambridge Shared Planning service is shared by Cambridge City and South Cambridgeshire councils. Weekly application lists are published (for example by parishes). A December 2020 scrutiny report documents earlier backlog problems — [SCDC Scrutiny Dec 2020](https://scambs.moderngov.co.uk/documents/s120122/Shared%20Planning%20Service%20Delivery%20Update%20to%20Scrutiny.pdf); [GCSP applications portal](https://applications.greatercambridgeplanning.org/online-applications/)
- From 23 July 2026 the new **Greater Cambridge Development Corporation** is expected to receive plan-making powers and decision powers over **major** applications through a later statutory instrument (see Section 5). Minor applications such as garden plots would appear to stay with the councils or GCSP — [Hansard Lords 22 Jul 2026](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Lords/2026-07-22/debates/BE03A8C0-8F8B-4BDC-9206-CA8AE78EB4BC/GreaterCambridgeDevelopmentCorporation(Establishment)Order2026); [Planning Resource](https://www.planningresource.co.uk/article/1960279/government-confirms-greater-cambridge-development-corporation-plan-making-decision-powers)

### Inferences
- Policy 52 and H/GL form a criteria-based "yes, if" policy, not a ban. Garden-plot and backland opportunities in CB1–CB5 remain possible but carry heavy discretion: character, retained garden size, trees, neighbour amenity, access and parking, and not blocking comprehensive development. The screening tool should score these factors (plot depth and width, frontage or access width, tree cover, conservation-area status, neighbouring backland precedent) rather than treat garden land as developable by default.
- Policy continuity from 2018 through the 2026 Reg 19 draft means precedent from recent Policy 52 decisions remains a good guide into the adoption of the new plan (likely 2027–28).
- With 17–18 conservation areas covering much of central and inner-suburban Cambridge, many CB1–CB5 plots face added heritage tests and the loss of some permitted development rights (for example on side extensions and cladding). Conservation-area status should be a hard screening layer.

### Gaps
- The full text of Policy 52's criteria could not be fetched to verify. From background knowledge (unverified in this session) they cover: form, height and layout fitting the surrounding pattern; sufficient retained garden and space around dwellings; neighbour amenity and privacy; adequate amenity space, access and parking; and no harm to comprehensive development of the wider area. Verify against the [Local Plan 2018 PDF](https://cambridge.gov.uk/media/6890/local-plan-2018.pdf).
- No data found on the number of applications for new dwellings in gardens or approval rates. GCSP's Authority Monitoring Report and a custom portal scrape would be needed.
- No data on the share of the city covered by conservation areas. The Cambridgeshire Insight dataset exists but was not readable.
- No current (2025–26) GCSP performance statistics (share decided in time, backlog, validation delays).
- Homes and jobs totals in the July 2026 Proposed Submission plan were not captured.

## 4. Constraints: water, wastewater, Green Belt, flood, nutrients

### Takeaway
Water is the defining constraint. The Environment Agency objected to major Greater Cambridge schemes from December 2022 onward, and by late 2025 had withdrawn support for already-planned development of about 9,000 homes and a cancer hospital. Relief depends on a Grafham Water transfer (about 26 Ml/d, around 2032), the Fens Reservoir (mid-to-late 2030s) and a government "water credits" offsetting scheme that is still in design. Anglian Water has also lodged wastewater holding objections tied to the Cambridge Water Recycling Centre. The water objections target large schemes; their effect on single-plot infill is not established.

### Cited Findings
- The EA **withdrew support for already approved development plans for 9,000 homes and a cancer hospital**, lodging objections on the basis that there is not enough water (Commons Water Scarcity debate, **9 Dec 2025**) — [Hansard: Water Scarcity 9 Dec 2025](https://hansard.parliament.uk/commons/2025-12-09/debates/39E79F6D-FED7-4CE2-8928-8CE379AD1572/WaterScarcity); [Cambridge Independent: EA objects to Cambridge Cancer Research Hospital over water](https://www.cambridgeindependent.co.uk/news/environment-agency-objects-to-new-cambridge-cancer-research-9361233/)

## 5. Growth plans: government ambitions, Development Corporation, big sites and what they mean for small-site investors

### Takeaway
Government ambition for Cambridge moved from the 2024 "Silicon Valley of Europe" / Case for Cambridge vision (Cambridge Delivery Group under Peter Freeman) to a statutory **Greater Cambridge Development Corporation**, established 23 July 2026 and chaired by Freeman, with plan-making and major-application powers to follow. Headline sites are Cambridge East (Homes England/Hill, 10,000+ homes, first phase from 2029), North East Cambridge (8,300+ homes, stalled after HIF funding was withdrawn in Aug 2025) and Waterbeach (4,500 homes, permitted). Big-site delivery is slow and water-constrained, which leaves the near-term market to existing stock and small sites.

### Inferences
- For small-site investors the growth agenda is a **medium-term demand tailwind** (jobs and population) with **limited near-term supply competition**. Cambridge East does not deliver homes until 2029 or later, North East Cambridge is unfunded and uncertain after August 2025, and water caps big-site delivery into the 2030s.
- The GCDC will likely take **major** applications (10+ dwellings) and strategic plan-making. Householder, minor infill and garden-plot applications in CB1–CB5 probably stay with GCSP, but the split depends on the forthcoming statutory instrument. Watch for the "gentle density" agenda turning into more permissive intensification or design-code policy for suburban plots, which would help garden or backland and replacement schemes.
- GCDC's remit to "unlock stalled and derelict land" suggests it may assemble or acquire brownfield sites. That could compete with or help small developers on edge-of-centre sites.
